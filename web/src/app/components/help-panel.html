How this works

Splitting costs between friends is rarely an even divide: one person books the flights, another covers dinner, and nobody ate the same number of pizza slices. This app records what was bought and who benefited, then works out the smallest set of payments that makes everyone square.

The five steps

All but the last happen in one grid, on the Split tab.

  1. People — everyone taking part is a column. + Add person, above the grid, makes one; type the name in its header.
  2. Expense sheets — one per check or category, each spanning its own block of rows. + Add expense sheet is above the grid too.
  3. Lines — type on the blank row at the end of a sheet's block to add one, with its amount.
  4. Sheet settings — click a sheet's cell for its currency, tax, tip, discount and who paid.
  5. Settle up — read off who pays whom.

Share ratios

Share values are relative, not amounts. Within a row they only mean something compared to each other, so the app never asks you to do arithmetic.

  • 1 1 1 — three people split a line evenly.
  • 2 2 — also an even split. The ratio is what counts, not the size.
  • 1 3 4 — an 8-slice pizza where one had 1 slice, another 3, the last 4.
  • Leave a cell empty for anyone who did not benefit from that line.

Tax, tip and discount are spread across the lines of their sheet in proportion to price, then follow the same share ratios.

Recording who paid

There are two ways, and you would normally use whichever fits the receipt:

  • Per sheet — click the sheet's cell on the Split tab and tick people under Paid by. They cover that whole sheet, split equally between them. This is the usual case: one person hands over a card for the whole check.
  • Per line — use the decimal part of a split cell. 1.2 means "owes one share, and already paid two shares' worth". That covers a camping trip where everyone brought something different.
The two cannot be mixed on the same sheet — if a sheet declares payers, its per-line payments are ignored and the app says so. And once anyone is recorded as paying, every priced line needs a payer, otherwise the balances silently understate what is owed.

Reading the balances

The row above the names is what each person's card should be charged. A figure in parentheses and red means the opposite: that person is owed money.

  • When nobody is marked as paying, the balances simply add up to the trip total — this is the "split the check" case.
  • When payers are recorded, the balances net to zero: every debt has a matching credit.

Settlement groups

Usually the simplest way to settle is for everyone to pay whoever is owed the most, and for that person to pay the rest. Sometimes the balances break into independent subsets that can each settle among themselves, which needs fewer payments. When that happens the app colours each group with a dot and lists the payments separately.

The odd cent

Rounding each person's balance to the nearest cent can leave the column a cent or two off the total. The app nudges as many people as needed by ±0.01 so the figures always reconcile exactly.

Different currencies

Each expense sheet can keep its own currency while the split happens in the trip's base currency. Both currency boxes can be typed into — “forint”, “huf” or “hungarian” all find the same one. Pick a currency in the sheet's editor and a rate appears:

  • Leave it alone to use the built-in rate.
  • Type your own to pin it — useful when you know what the card actually charged.
  • Clear the box to go back to the built-in rate.

Rates are a fixed snapshot bundled with the app, not a live feed.

Keeping several splits

The Splits tab lists everything you have saved, most recently edited first — a trip, last week's dinner, the flat's bills. Open one to work on it; the others are left exactly as they were.

  • New split starts an empty one.
  • Duplicate copies a split, which is handy when the same people split costs the same way every month.
  • Delete removes one for good. Deleting your last split leaves a fresh empty one, so there is always something to work on.

Once the list grows, the search box narrows it. It looks at more than the name — the people, the expense sheets and the items are all searchable, so “sarah”, “peruvian” or “plane tickets” each find the right split even when it is still called New Split. Several words narrow further, and each result says what it matched.

Sort by reorders the list: by when you last worked on a split, when you added it, its name, or its total. The choice is remembered, and applies to search results too.

Where the data lives

In this browser, on this device. Splits are saved as you type, so closing the tab or reloading picks up where you left off. Nothing is sent anywhere — there is no account and no server.

Two consequences worth knowing:

  • Another device, another browser, or a private window will not see these splits. There is no sharing yet.
  • Clearing your browsing data clears them too.

If you have the app open in more than one tab, they stay in step, and each tab remembers which split it was showing — you can keep two splits open side by side. Should you happen to be editing the same split in two tabs at once, the app asks which version to keep rather than picking for you.

Moving splits somewhere else

Export downloads a split as a JSON file: people, every expense sheet, every share. Export all on the Splits tab puts the whole library in one file. Either is the way to keep a backup, move splits to another device, or send one to somebody who can open it with Import.

Importing adds to your splits — it never overwrites what is already saved. A file imported twice gives you two independent copies.
